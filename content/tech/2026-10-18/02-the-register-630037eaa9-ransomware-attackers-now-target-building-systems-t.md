Cybercriminals are attacking building automation systems that control HVAC, door locks, and other physical infrastructure. A French grocery chain was hit Sunday, with refrigeration units spiking temperatures and sparking fires. A Canadian hospital's doors and access controls went offline. These attacks cause real-world harm, not just data theft.

Building automation systems appeal to extortionists because they create immediate pressure on executives to pay. Physical consequences like locked doors or failed climate control are harder to tolerate than encrypted files. One attacker locked an office building entirely; police had to break windows to let staff in.

Security vulnerabilities compound the risk. 73 percent of organisations have building systems with known exploitable gaps. 91 percent use insecure protocols. These systems get patched less frequently than IT infrastructure because downtime can affect operations.

AI now lets criminals without deep OT expertise understand obscure industrial protocols and legacy systems. Attackers previously avoided certain systems from fear of unknown consequences. AI removes that barrier, letting them make targeted changes—like altering weight distribution on an aircraft.
