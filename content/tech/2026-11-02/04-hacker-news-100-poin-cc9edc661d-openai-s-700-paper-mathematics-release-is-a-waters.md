Frank Calegari, in a guest post on Terence Tao's blog, calls OpenAI's 6 October release of more than 700 research papers a watershed in the history of mathematics. AI, he writes, is now undeniably capable of groundbreaking contributions across many areas of the subject. The scale has limits. The dump made barely a dent on the general problems of functoriality and reciprocity in the Langlands program. Despite some tremendous ideas, the papers are atrociously written, break norms on attribution, and some are wrong.

The post is a study in how an expert community responds when machine-generated claims arrive faster than it can check them. Calegari quotes Hugo Duminil-Copin, who found that every major open problem he had publicly mentioned in his career, down to those in grant proposals, was claimed as proved by the announcement. His prescription is to spend effort where mathematicians have control, on hiring, publishing, and training students, rather than on the behaviour of trillion-dollar companies.

The precedent he reaches for is the classification of finite simple groups. Dan Gorenstein announced a program in 1972 and declared the classification done in 1983, somewhat prematurely, with the quasithin case left to Aschbacher and Smith for another two decades. The theorem was a success but cooled interest in group theory, even though rich questions remained. Calegari wants the equivalent of the revised classification by Gorenstein, Lyons, and Solomon, and hiring that rewards people who keep exploring rather than declaring subjects done.

He also sees an opening. AI, he hopes, can free mathematicians from the straitjacket of the first natural conjecture they cannot prove. He admits distress at how often mathematicians cannot explain to anyone slightly outside their field why their work is interesting. Two caveats. This is an opinion piece, so the quality claims are his assessment. And Tao notes the post itself was converted from a different file format using AI.
