Boeing dismantled airframe WH007, line number 1611, after deciding that structural modifications needed to meet current certification standards would cost more than building a new 777-9. The jet had sat in storage at Everett for six years, from its July 2019 rollout, without engines attached. A thrust link failure during 2024 testing forced GE9X mount redesigns across the fleet.

The write-off matters to anyone watching the 777X programme, because it signals that the roughly 30 to 40 early-build airframes already produced may be too expensive to finish. Emirates has refused its first ten aircraft outright, calling them only fit for 'baked bean cans', and Lufthansa is negotiating to reject some frames and seek compensation on others. United Airlines has also ruled out picking up discounted units, despite long wait times for new widebodies.

Early-build jets carry extra structural reinforcement that permanently raises their operating empty weight and fuel burn, and their cabins hold 2019-era wiring and seat tracks that would need full replacement. With secondary market buyers unwilling to absorb those costs and cargo conversion ruled out, more airframes could face the same fate as line number 1611, deepening Boeing's already reported 15 billion dollars in cumulative 777X programme charges.
