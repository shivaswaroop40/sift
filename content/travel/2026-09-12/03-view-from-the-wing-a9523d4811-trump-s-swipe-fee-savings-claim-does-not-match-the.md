Donald Trump told the Republican midterm convention that cutting credit card swipe fees would save the average family $1,200 a year. The bill he has endorsed, Dick Durbin’s Credit Card Competition Act, makes no such claim. It would require banks with more than $100 billion in assets to enable at least two unaffiliated networks on credit cards, letting merchants choose which routes each transaction. Amex and Discover are exempt.

The $1,200 figure comes from the merchant campaign for regulation. Bill co-author Senator Roger Marshall says the average family pays nearly that because swipe fees are embedded in retail prices, echoing a National Retail Federation claim. But the federation’s own page estimates total annual savings of $15 billion nationwide, one-seventh split between businesses and consumers. If all of it reached consumers, that would be $174 per family, and nothing requires businesses to lower prices.

The law would shift payment networks from spending to attract cardholders to spending to attract merchants. That transfer would cut rewards and purchase protections. The New York Fed, looking at 550 million card accounts, found issuers receive 1.82% of purchase volume and spend 1.57% on rewards. Countries that regulate interchange have lower sign-up bonuses and lower or capped rewards. The Reserve Bank of Australia predicted its rules would mean lower rewards, fewer card features and higher fees.

The bill also makes extending credit less profitable, which means less credit, especially for marginal borrowers, and pushes them toward payday lending. Rewards are crucial for air travel volume and affordability. Delta says it is building up Austin flights because of its American Express deal. Southwest moved into Hawaii to give cardmembers a reason to earn. Cut rewards and you get fewer flights and more expensive seats.
