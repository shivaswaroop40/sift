A chapter by Embry-Riddle's Jules Yimga, "The Price of Punctuality", links schedule padding to higher fares. It combines 2019 flight time data with the US government's ticket price survey and compares published schedules against percentiles of actual flight times. The link is strongest when measured against the faster end of actual times, and it appears in both competitive and concentrated markets.

The author of the post argues that this is not simply passengers paying more for a slower trip. Longer scheduled times let airlines hold connections, as United does with ConnectionSaver, and still arrive on time. By one estimate flights are 8% longer than before, which adds about 10 minutes to a two-hour flight. Congested airspace and airports are the main causes.

The practical effect is more dependable connections. American's Dallas-Fort Worth overhaul from April added scheduled time and moved from nine daily banks of flights to thirteen. Bookings for 55 to 84 minute connections ran 8% above its model, while 40 to 54 minute connections ran 4% below. Passengers seem to prefer the longer, safer gap.

There are caveats. The abstract only allows that reliability may be a service customers value. Yimga's earlier work put higher prices down mainly to higher costs, not higher margins. The post also says too much slack is possible, since an airline can look punctual just by promising a later arrival.
