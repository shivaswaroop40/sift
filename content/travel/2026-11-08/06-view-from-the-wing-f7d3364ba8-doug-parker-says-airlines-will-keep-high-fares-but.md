Doug Parker, the former American Airlines chief executive, told the Airlines Confidential podcast that flying was never a commodity product and that airlines can retain higher fares once fuel gets cheaper. His example was the window seat against the middle seat, which customers value differently. Several airlines made the same promise on recent earnings calls.

For travellers, the question is whether fares fall as fuel does. Airlines price by what passengers will pay, not cost plus a markup. Cheaper fuel makes marginal flights worth operating, which adds seats, and those seats need filling, which pushes fares down. Schedules change slowly, so any shift lags. If airlines could simply choose high fares, they would have charged them before fuel spiked.

The author counters that Parker's own strategy made American feel like a commodity. In 2018 he told employees the airline worked 'really hard to match our service to our competitors'. American moved its first class meal threshold to flights of 2 hours 45 minutes in 2014, pulled business seats from its 777-200s and 787-8s, and closed lounges in Buffalo, San Juan, Hartford and Greensboro.

Delta moved the other way. By December 2015, 57% of its domestic first class passengers had paid something to sit there, up from 45% in 2014. American is now adding premium seats and improving lounges, several years behind rivals. Loyalty schemes were meant to differentiate similar flights, and a better cabin can win years of credit card spending.
