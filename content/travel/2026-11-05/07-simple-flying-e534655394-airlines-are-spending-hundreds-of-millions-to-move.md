Legacy long-haul carriers are tearing 2-2-2 and 2-3-2 business class out of their 787-9 fleets and installing 1-2-1 suites. Air India is spending $400 million refitting its 787-8s and 787-9s, adding premium economy alongside the new business seats. LATAM has committed $360 million to replacing 2-2-2 frames with staggered 1-2-1 Recaro R7 suites. British Airways swapped a 42-seat 2-3-2 Club World cabin for 38 Club Suites with sliding privacy doors.

The main driver is the step-over problem. In a 2-2-2 grid, a window passenger must climb over a sleeping aisle neighbour to reach the lavatory. Corporate travel managers now treat direct aisle access as non-negotiable in preferred-carrier agreements, and airlines holding old layouts lose high-yield volume to competitors on the same routes. For premium long-haul buyers, direct aisle access has become the baseline rather than a luxury.

Seat counts do not always fall. Japan Airlines moved from 44 Sky Suite I seats in 2-2-2 to 52 Sky Suite III reverse-herringbone suites in 1-2-1 on its 203-seat 787-9, gaining eight seats by angling seats toward the windows and overlapping footwells under side consoles. The catch is cabin width. The 787 fuselage is 18 feet across, 38 cm narrower than a 777-300ER, so four suites per row mean narrower hip room and tapered footwells.

Carriers are also expanding premium economy to offset the business class floor space given up. Installed in 2-3-2 on the 787, it earns high revenue per square foot by drawing budget-conscious business travellers and premium leisure flyers who will not pay full business fares. That dual-cabin strategy lets airlines shrink the forward cabin without losing overall revenue, and it widens the choice for travellers who buy premium economy instead.
