JetBlue's $2 billion in bonds secured by its TrueBlue frequent flyer programme have fallen to 72 cents on the dollar since their August 2024 issue, with yields to maturity now around 19%. The airline still holds $2.2 billion in unrestricted cash plus a $600 million undrawn credit line, but faces $8.5 billion in total debt and finance leases.

For independent travellers relying on JetBlue routes or TrueBlue status, the airline's financial stress matters because a restructuring or sale could alter routes, partnerships and loyalty programme value. A bankruptcy filing could happen before the 2029 debt wall, triggered by continued losses, refinancing failures or credit card processor disputes.

The airline's problems stem from poor operational performance rather than fuel costs alone. JetBlue lost $141 million before interest in the second quarter despite 14.5% revenue growth, a deficit unexcplained by fuel prices that competitors managed profitably.

Creditors have contractual protections over the TrueBlue asset, including quarterly liquidity minimums and debt-service coverage tests starting September 2026. A restructuring could preserve the airline's assets while reorganising ownership and debt, rather than forcing a full shutdown.
