A traveller reports on a midwinter stay in Abisko, a small village in Swedish Lapland reached by train on the Stockholm to Narvik line, after a three-day rail journey from London. The sun stayed below the horizon for the whole trip. Darkness had fallen by 2pm on the first day, and the stars were out by mid-afternoon the next.

Abisko sits in a microclimate known as the blue hole, where the local geography produces frequent clear skies. That makes it one of the most reliable places on Earth to see the aurora borealis. The writer saw green displays on two evenings, one from the village itself. The trip also works without a car, since the station, a handful of lodges, a supermarket and hiking paths sit within walking distance.

Plans can still change. High winds stopped the 20-minute cable car to the Abisko Sky Station, a viewpoint 900 metres above sea level, so a guide took the group to an outcrop above Lake Torneträsk for a second aurora display. The account is experiential rather than practical. It says little about costs, booking lead times or how many nights improve the odds of clear skies.
