American Airlines spent years cutting its domestic product, with less legroom, thinner seat padding and no seatback screens, on the theory that better products just get copied. Roughly 21 months into a premium pivot, it is trying to win passengers back from United and Delta while losing money, carrying heavy debt and facing higher fuel costs. It is a race to show results before Wall Street gets impatient.

For frequent flyers, this explains why US cabin quality, apps and food are improving again. United's chief executive Scott Kirby says even a temporary edge, such as two or three years of Starlink internet, is long enough to move loyalty and credit card spending. He now says United will consciously spend more for customers every year, betting that brand-loyal flyers will repay it.

Delta's two-decade lead came from operations and positioning, not just cabins. In the 2011 slot swap, US Airways gave Delta 132 daily LaGuardia slot pairs for 42 at Washington National and $66.5 million, letting Delta build its New York business and overtake American in cobrand card spend. The author argues Delta's edge is eroding, and its 767s from New York are arguably the worst product across the Atlantic.

United's own reversals show the stakes. It cut domestic first class meals on flights under four hours outside the dinner window, then restored them after complaints. It now invests in coach, where most passengers sit, because international business buyers form their views largely from domestic trips. A good app also keeps flyers loyal even after rivals match the cabin.
