The NTSB's final report on a May 2024 accident found that severe winds damaged a Southwest Boeing 737 MAX 8 while it was parked overnight in New Orleans. The damage hit the standby rudder power control unit's mounting structure and its connection to the vertical stabilizer. With no visible external damage, no inspection followed. The jet flew 44 flights over nine days before its crew felt a Dutch roll at 34,000 feet on a Phoenix to Oakland service. It landed safely.

The report shows how a damaged aircraft can stay in service despite checks. A scheduled maintenance test in Houston two days earlier should have caught the fault. Hangar footage showed the rudder moving right but not left, with nobody at the rear to observe it, yet records showed the test as passed. Boeing's manual required inspections after extreme winds but set no wind speed threshold, and Southwest had no procedure to alert maintenance control.

Both companies have since tightened rules. Boeing's revised 737 manual, issued in May 2025, requires an extreme high-wind inspection when ground winds reach 65 knots, still below the 73-knot gusts recorded in New Orleans that night. Airbus uses an 80-knot threshold for most aircraft. Southwest inspected all 231 of its 737 MAX jets in June 2024, found no similar damage, and now monitors weather reports for winds above 65 knots.
