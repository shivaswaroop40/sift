American and United have configured their Airbus A321XLRs with far more business and premium economy seats than Iberia, which already flies ballast on some lightly booked transatlantic flights according to a new report flagged by blogger Enilria. United's jet has 150 seats with 20 Polaris business class berths; American's has 155 with 20 business class suites; Iberia's has 182 with 14 business class and 168 economy.

Premium seats are heavier than coach seats, so a plane can be below its weight limit yet still nose-heavy if the front cabin is full and the rear is empty. The XLR's rear centre fuel tank, drawn from first, shifts the centre of gravity forward as fuel burns. Too much forward weight can make nose-up flare for landing harder, so load planners may add ballast, move bags aft, or reseat passengers.

Iberia has reportedly loaded ballast at the back of its XLRs and even moved business class passengers into economy to get the balance right. The author is unconvinced this will push airlines to slash economy fares, since cheap seats can cannibalise premium revenue, and questions whether United's rear snack bar is ballast-related given its non-XLR A321neo Coastliner has one too.

The A321XLR is meant to open thin long-haul markets where a widebody cannot be filled. Its lower trip cost but higher per-passenger cost depends on selling enough premium seats. Ballast adds fuel burn and takes hold space, though less weight is needed than equivalent passengers and it can be placed further aft for more leverage.
