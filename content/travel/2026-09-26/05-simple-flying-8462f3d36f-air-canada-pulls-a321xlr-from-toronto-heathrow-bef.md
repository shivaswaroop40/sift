Air Canada had scheduled its Airbus A321XLR to fly between Toronto Pearson and London Heathrow from 31 August to 23 October, with five daily rotations in mind. By the May schedule change, the carrier replaced the narrowbody with Boeing 777-200LRs in September and 787-8s and 787-9s in October, so the A321XLR never operated the route.

The A321XLR is configured with 182 seats, against 255 to 300 on the 787-8, 787-9 and 777-200LR that took over. Heathrow is heavily slot-constrained, so carriers use each slot to carry as many paying passengers and as much cargo as possible, making a smaller narrowbody uneconomic on the busy Toronto link.

Heathrow's slot rules shaped the outcome as much as Air Canada's planning. The fifth rotation was meant to be a morning day-tripper, but slot availability limited how and when the flight could operate, reinforcing the case for keeping widebodies on the trunk route for now.

The A321XLR remains in use on thinner transatlantic and domestic services, with six in the fleet and 30 on order. Whether it returns to Toronto-Heathrow depends on slot availability and whether the airline judges the capacity trade-off worth it.
