Doug Parker, former American Airlines chief executive, told the Airlines Confidential podcast that executives do not signal competitors, saying it "absolutely, positively does not happen". Yet he described airlines enlisting analysts to ask a rival why it is doing something. A Justice Department merger complaint described a 2010 email in which he sent his concerns about Delta's expansion straight to Delta's chief executive. American paid $45 million in 2018 to settle a class action alleging coordinated capacity restraint to lift fares, while denying wrongdoing.

The pressure matters for fares and routes. The 2018 case alleged that holding down capacity raises fares, and deterred expansion works the same way. United's Scott Kirby used his January earnings call to promise a costly response to American's growth at Chicago O'Hare, wanting analysts to ask American why it would fight. American may still judge a bigger Chicago network worth it for corporate contracts, connections and AAdvantage card spending.

The mechanics run through fare filings. Airlines publish fares via the Airline Tariff Publishing Company, and pricing teams use fare basis codes to catch a rival's eye, including discount fares in a competitor's home market coded to end in FU. In the American and JetBlue litigation, the government described JetBlue answering a $139 American Boston to Los Angeles fare with $139 fares on five routes serving American hubs, and planning a fare filing to signal a dispute over $44 Boston to Philadelphia pricing in February 2020.

Parker's broader point about hubs holds. An incumbent with connecting traffic, corporate contracts and loyal local customers is hard to challenge without any agreement. But natural advantages and efforts to discourage competition can coexist. In 1982 American's Bob Crandall phoned Braniff's Howard Putnam, proposing Braniff raise fares twenty percent and promising to match. Putnam recorded the call and gave it to the government, and the Fifth Circuit reinstated an attempted-monopolization case.
