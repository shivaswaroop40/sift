The US Essential Air Service programme, created in 1978 as a ten-year transition for small communities after airline deregulation, now costs $680 million a year in fiscal 2026. Congress made it permanent in 1996 and has steadily increased funding, with the 2024 FAA reauthorisation authorising roughly $340m to $350m annually through 2028. Spending has risen from $28.5m in 1988 to $286m in 2018 and $680m today.

For independent travellers, the programme shapes which small-city routes exist and at what price. The Pueblo to Denver service, for instance, is forecast to fill only 22% of seats and receives $6.84m a year. The 2024 reauthorisation tripled the per-passenger subsidy ceiling to $650 inside 175 miles of a hub and suspended eligibility tests for fiscal 2026.

The article lists several awards near larger airports: Lancaster ($5.17m, 32 miles from Harrisburg), Owensboro ($5.83m, 43 miles from Evansville), Decatur ($6.43m, 46 miles from Champaign), Muskegon ($7.28m, 51 miles from Grand Rapids), and Hot Springs ($3.16m, 58 miles from Little Rock). Victoria, Texas receives about $6.82m and has offered residents an extra $100 to fly.

Senator Susan Collins highlighted $513.6m for Maine airports; Senator Kirsten Gillibrand touted roughly $514m for New York routes. The author argues these are concentrated local benefits dispersed across all taxpayers, including airline passengers, who tend to be higher-income.
