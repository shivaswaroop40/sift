The US Federal Trade Commission has opened an inquiry into whether nine large companies, including Delta, United and JetBlue, set 'personalised' prices. The tactic means showing one customer a higher fare than another for the same flight, based on data held about them. The carriers deny it. Airlines for America, the industry trade group, says its members rely on marketplace conditions and consumer demand, as they always have.

The stakes for travellers are clear. Airlines already hold plenty of personal data, from the details security rules require at booking to the profiles built through loyalty programmes. An FTC official said the agency is less concerned about personalised pricing itself than about companies doing it without telling consumers. Some differentiated pricing is already open, including award discounts for Delta and United cardholders and targeted upgrade offers.

Hard evidence of fare personalisation remains thin. Delta, scrutinised by lawmakers last year over a third-party AI tool used to help set prices, insists it never used customers' private information. A lawsuit against JetBlue rests largely on a deleted social media comment that suggested clearing cookies, which the airline called a mistake by a customer service agent. Garrett Scott, an economist who studies airline pricing, is 'not 100% convinced' it is happening.

The line may be blurring, though. Mark Tremblay, a pricing expert at the University of Nevada, Las Vegas, argues that raising holiday fares in a week when data shows wealthier travellers book amounts to personalised pricing in disguise. The FTC has ordered all nine firms to hand over details of their pricing tactics, including whether fares can change with loyalty programme participation. Uber, Lyft, Walmart, Target, DoorDash and Instacart are also covered.
