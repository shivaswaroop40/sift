The Airbus A321XLR has split its operators after nearly two years in service. Iberia, which flew the first commercial service in November 2024, says the type burns up to 40% less fuel than widebody models. Qantas flies 197 to 200 recliner seats, mostly domestically, and values the payload as much as the range. Wizz Air converted 36 orders to the standard A321neo, keeping 11. JetBlue deferred 11 of its 13 and sold its first two to Aegean, which rejected them.

The split matters for anyone booking thin long-haul routes. United, which ordered 50 A321XLRs to replace its Boeing 757-200 fleet, says the jet cannot reliably fly Newark to Italy as marketed. Air Canada has raised concerns about range and payload in hot weather and at challenging airfields. Regulator-mandated fire protection in the rear centre tank added weight, and Airbus lifted maximum takeoff weight to 101.5 tonnes to offset it.

The brochure range of 4,700 nautical miles assumes favourable winds, a light configuration and an optional auxiliary centre tank, which United did not order. The longest A321XLR service in operation, IndiGo's Mumbai to Amsterdam flight, spans 3,707 nautical miles. Heavy lie-flat seats also hurt the type more than a widebody, because the airframe is smaller and lighter overall.

The Wizz Air and JetBlue retreats had causes beyond the aircraft itself. Wizz Air's orders were mostly for its Abu Dhabi subsidiary, which shut in September 2025, and five delivered jets are now stored, stripped of engines amid the Pratt & Whitney GTF shortage. JetBlue's deferral formed part of a wider push of 44 A321neos to 2030 and beyond. It sold its first two to avoid a two-aircraft orphan fleet, and Aegean rejected them after seat delays pushed delivery past the summer season.
