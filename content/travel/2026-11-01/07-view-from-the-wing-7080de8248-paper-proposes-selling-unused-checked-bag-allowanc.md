A paper in the Review of Economic Design proposes a market for transferable checked baggage rights. Passengers could trade weight above a baseline allowance, within a capacity limit set by the airline. Those who value carrying more would buy rights from those who check nothing. The initial allocation of rights decides who receives the money.

The idea appeals to travellers with an airline credit card or elite status who often fly with just a carry-on. That unused allowance currently earns nothing. But airlines price the benefit on breakage. Of 100 passengers entitled to one free bag, typically only 60 check one. The unused allowance is option value, not waste. If every allowance became sellable, the benefit would cost airlines more, and fares, elite perks and card bundles would be repriced.

The airline's cost depends on what the buyer would otherwise do. A passenger already paying to check the same suitcase simply shifts fee revenue from the airline to the seller. An extra suitcase adds weight, handling and fuel. Moving a carry-on into the hold barely changes total weight and may speed boarding. Any transfer would run through the airline, with the buyer checking a bag under their own name.

A limited version already exists. Ryanair lets passengers on the same booking pool their checked-baggage allowances. Two travellers who each bought 20 kilograms can split that as 15 and 25, subject to the per-bag maximum. Wider trading would force airlines to revisit the bundle's price, and some marginal flights might stop making economic sense.
