Delta now sells domestic first class without advance seat selection or same-day changes, charging $300 to alter bookings. United's version launches this autumn with one checked bag instead of two, no voluntary changes, and reduced mileage earning. JetBlue's BlueFirst follows suit with similar restrictions.

For independent travellers, this matters because first class has historically sold the promise of fewer hassles. When airlines add fees and restrictions to premium cabins, they risk eroding the aspirational appeal that justifies the price.

The budget carriers already unbundle their front seats from services. The difference is that Delta and United still offer meals and maintain premium brands, which partially shields them. But if customers start seeing first class as just a bigger chair with separate fees, rather than a seamless escape, demand will shift.
