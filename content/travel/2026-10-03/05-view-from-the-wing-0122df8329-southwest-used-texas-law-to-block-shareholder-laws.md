Southwest Airlines adopted a rule requiring shareholders to own 3% of the company to sue derivatively, worth $609 million in stock at the time. A shareholder with 100 shares sued directors for breaching fiduciary duty by introducing bag fees despite forecasting $300 million in annual losses. The case was dismissed under the new Texas threshold.

The airline had previously argued that charging for bags would reduce ticket sales by more than the fee revenue gained. Investors planning routes around free baggage policies need to know Southwest's business model shift is now locked in by investor commitments and cannot easily reverse, even if the financial case weakens.

Elliott Investment Management, the activist investor that pushed the changes, began selling shares as the strategy took hold. Two Elliott-backed directors left the board in February. Management is committed to the strategy through pricing, credit card benefits and seat assignments.
