Oregon State University researchers have patented a water-based precipitation route for separating zirconium from hafnium, replacing the flammable organic solvent used in the only two US industrial-scale separation plants. The mixture combines dissolved zircon ore, thiocyanate ligands that bind the metal ions, and choline, an inexpensive food-grade additive, to drive hafnium-rich species out of solution.

The process was first reported by the group. Reported separation factors reached 33, against an industry standard of six to seven, and the researchers say precipitation competes with solvent extraction. They attribute the selectivity to atomic-level control of how zirconium and hafnium thiocyanate species assemble before they precipitate.

Only ATI Specialty Alloys & Components and Westinghouse Electric currently perform the separation in the US, each consuming millions of pounds of solvent a year and losing about 4% of it to the air as noxious vapour. Replacing that loop with a low-energy aqueous route could cut both emissions and solvent make-up costs if the route scales. The team notes remaining questions and milestones before commercial deployment.

Hafnium-free zirconium is required for nuclear reactor cladding, while pure hafnium is needed for semiconductor manufacturing, so supply depends on the difficult separation these two plants handle.
