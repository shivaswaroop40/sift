A new design framework links the operating window of a flexible chemical production subsystem directly to intermittent renewable supply within a single capacity sizing and scheduling model. It maps how equipment design variables set the feasible operating boundaries, then co-optimises equipment size, operating range and storage dispatch.

In a wind-driven methanol case study, the method cut total annual cost by 26.38% versus a conventional economy-oriented design and by 43.59% versus a purely flexibility-oriented strategy. Storage capacity requirements fell because the process itself absorbed more of the wind variability through tighter operating-window matching.

The work shows that equipment-level physical constraints narrow the feasible optimisation space, so decoupling flexibility from capacity sizing leaves money on the table. Practitioners designing electrofuels or other renewable-fed chemicals can use the approach to reduce storage capex while keeping throughput stable.

The source is a single case study and an abstract-level description, so the savings are demonstrated for one wind-methanol configuration. Generalisability to other chemistries, shorter storage durations, or multi-product sites is not addressed in the available text.
