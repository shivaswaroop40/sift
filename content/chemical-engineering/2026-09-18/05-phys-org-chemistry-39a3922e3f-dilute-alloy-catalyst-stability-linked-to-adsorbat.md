University of Michigan researchers built a design rulebook for dilute alloy catalysts, which contain 1% or less of an active dopant metal dispersed in an inert host. The work, published in the Journal of the American Chemical Society, targets the thermal instability that has limited industrial use of these materials.

The team tested gold nanoparticles dotted with platinum in ethylene hydrogenation and carbon monoxide oxidation from 50°C to 250°C. Ethylene hydrogenation lost activity above 100°C because the weakly bound ethylene could not anchor platinum atoms to the surface, letting entropy drive them into the gold bulk. Strongly bound carbon monoxide pinned platinum in place and prevented deactivation.

Atomic simulations across gold, silver and copper hosts with iridium, palladium and platinum dopants showed that miscibility controls stability. Platinum and palladium mix readily with the host and tend to deactivate, while iridium resists mixing. A synthesised gold-iridium alloy showed no deactivation up to 250°C in either test reaction.

The findings give two practical rules: choose a strongly binding adsorbate for the target reaction, and pair the host with a dopant metal that resists mixing, to keep the active dopant on the surface under operating conditions.
