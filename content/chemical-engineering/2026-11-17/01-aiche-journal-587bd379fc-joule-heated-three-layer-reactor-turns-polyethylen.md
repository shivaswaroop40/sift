Researchers describe a three-layer Joule-heated reactor that melts, transports and catalytically cracks polyolefin waste in one unit. Transient electrical pulses create a short-lived hot catalytic zone, while the layers beneath stay thermally buffered. Light products leave quickly, which limits secondary cracking.

Under optimised conditions, low-density polyethylene gave a C1 to C5 hydrocarbon yield of 96.0 wt%. Ethylene molar selectivity was 49.4% of the quantified C1 to C5 gases, and specific ethylene yield was 14.3 mmol per gram. The authors say the approach also works with other polyolefins and post-consumer plastics.

Selectivity and secondary cracking are the usual limits on direct plastics-to-olefins routes. This design tackles both through heat and transport control rather than catalyst change alone. Ethylene selectivity below 50% still means a substantial mix of other light gases to separate.

The life-cycle comparison is against PE-to-naphtha followed by steam cracking. Within the authors' defined boundary, cumulative energy demand falls by about 47.7% and greenhouse-gas emissions by 36.5%. These are boundary-dependent figures from a lab-scale study, and the abstract gives no scale-up data.
