A University of Sydney team has directly observed hydrogen dissolved in the spaces between metal atoms, the interstitial state. Led by Julie Cairney and published in Nature, the work used cryogenic atom probe tomography on several alloys, substituting deuterium for hydrogen to limit losses during sample preparation. Deuterium concentrated preferentially in an ordered L12 phase of a multi-principal element alloy. Electron microscopy and theoretical calculations confirmed the atoms sit between metal atoms rather than only at defects.

The result matters for anyone specifying materials for hydrogen service. Hydrogen entering pipelines and storage vessels can embrittle otherwise strong metals and drive unexpected cracking. Hydrides and hydrogen trapped at defects had been observed before, but the dissolved form was understood only in theory. Knowing that composition and atomic arrangement control where hydrogen resides gives a basis for designing more hydrogen-resistant alloys.

Treat the result as foundational rather than a design rule. The paper is titled 'Towards observing interstitial hydrogen in solution in metal lattices', and the evidence is described as consistent with interstitial hydrogen in solid solution. The team has not yet linked these sites to deformation or cracking under stress. That work, on ultra-high-strength steels, is the stated next step.
