University of Tokyo chemists have developed a method called concentration-dependent yield analysis (CYAN) that uses machine learning to extract rate constants from yield data already collected during reaction optimisation. The tool augments sparse yield datasets, then fits them to rate equations chosen by the chemist to give kinetic information for each step of a reaction.

For practising engineers, the appeal is that a single set of optimisation runs can serve both to find the best conditions and to probe mechanism, removing the need for separate time-course kinetic experiments. The authors stress that CYAN complements rather than replaces time-course work when precise rates are needed.

In a test on a nickel-mediated ring-forming reaction, CYAN showed that nickel both accelerated the desired cyclisation and slowed a competing pathway, an effect the team describe as a counter-intuitive template effect that restricts one route rather than accelerating the other.

The method also worked on data from a paper published 176 years ago, producing a rate constant consistent with a modern measurement, which suggests historical datasets could be reanalysed for hidden kinetic information.
