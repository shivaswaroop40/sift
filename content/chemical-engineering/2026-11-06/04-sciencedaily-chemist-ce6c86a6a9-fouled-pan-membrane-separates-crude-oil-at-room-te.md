Researchers at KAIST and Georgia Tech have shown that a bare porous polyacrylonitrile membrane can separate crude oil at room temperature. As crude flows through, heavier hydrocarbons deposit on the pore walls and narrow the channels to under 2 nanometres. The fouled membrane then passes light fractions such as naphtha, gasoline and kerosene while holding back heavier components. The work appears in Nature.

The uncoated PAN membrane gave crude oil permeation rates about 23 times higher than previous leading separation membranes, and ran with stable performance for 28 consecutive days. Conventional distillation heats crude above 350°C and consumes roughly 1,100 TWh per year worldwide. The team proposes installing the membrane as a modular unit ahead of existing distillation equipment, so refineries keep much of their current plant.

Process simulations project energy consumption down 31.6%, carbon dioxide emissions down 37.6%, cooling water use down 20.7% and operating costs down 36%. Adoption across Korea's refining and petrochemical industries could avoid about 10 million tonnes of greenhouse gas emissions per year. These are simulation projections, not savings measured in operating refineries.
