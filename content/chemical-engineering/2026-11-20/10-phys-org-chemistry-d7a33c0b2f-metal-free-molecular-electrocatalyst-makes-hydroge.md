Chemists at Rutgers University–Newark and the University of Bonn report an electrocatalyst that reduces oxygen to hydrogen peroxide without metals or hydrogen gas. It is a nitrogen-rich organic molecule of the Cp N3 class. The feed is oxygen, an electric current and a proton source. More than 99% of the applied charge went to the product, with very high turnover frequencies. The work appears in Nature Communications.

The route avoids the palladium used in the anthraquinone process, which dominates production of several million tonnes a year, and it avoids handling flammable hydrogen. For process designers, it points to a possible decentralised, electrically driven alternative. That remains a long-term prospect, as the authors say further development is needed before industrial use.

The mechanism is unusual. A C-H bond in the ligand is as strong as the O-H bond in the hydroperoxyl radical, so it can transfer hydrogen to oxygen. Quantum chemical calculations, checked against experiment, support a radical rebound pathway. The radical re-binds to the catalyst before peroxide is released. This behaviour has mainly been seen in transition-metal complexes.

Open issues are basic. Catalyst stability and lifetime must be tested over longer runs and larger scale. The reaction also needs to move into water, which would serve as solvent and proton source. The Bonn group plans calculations on catalyst variants for the Rutgers team to test. No cost or scale-up data are given.
