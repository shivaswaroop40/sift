Researchers at Ohio State University report in Nature Chemistry that turning up the voltage on electrochemical CO-to-methanol conversion does not keep improving the rate. They mounted metal catalysts on carbon nanotubes and varied the applied electricity. At lower voltages, electron transfer limits the reaction. At higher voltages, the limit shifts to how quickly carbon monoxide adsorbs onto the catalyst. A cobalt catalyst held CO more strongly than the other metals tested.

The surrounding electrolyte matters too. Its composition and how fast the interfacial environment reorganises to accommodate the molecule both govern how quickly CO adsorbs. For electrolysers turning CO, and the CO2 it originates from, into methanol, this means raising voltage alone will not raise throughput indefinitely. Catalyst and electrolyte design must target the adsorption step once the cell runs at high overpotential.

The work is mechanistic rather than a process demonstration. The team has not yet tested how the system performs over long runs, whether the findings extend to other electrochemical reactions, or how it compares with conventional methanol synthesis. Those studies are planned. The results are a foundation for catalyst and electrolyser design, not a ready process.
