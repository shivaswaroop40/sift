A vendor-authored article in Chemical Engineering argues that the case for predictive maintenance in the process industries rests on what an unplanned failure truly costs. Deloitte puts unplanned downtime across industrial manufacturing at roughly $50 billion a year, and poor maintenance strategies can cut plant capacity by 5-20%. In mining and heavy industry, unplanned downtime runs $50,000 to $200,000 per hour. US Department of Energy data show predictive maintenance programmes cut maintenance costs 25-30% and downtime 35-45%.

The economics differ from site to site. Where a single failure can halt a high-value process for hours, monitoring is easy to justify. Where an established preventive programme already works and failure consequences are small, it may not be. The central advice is to start with the failure mode rather than the technology. Pressure changes point to line clogging, motor temperature to overheating, and vibration patterns to mechanical wear.

The worked example is a pump feeding a process stream in mining or cement. Material builds up in the suction line and restricts flow. Pressure monitoring flags the change early, so a flush can be scheduled into the next planned outage rather than forcing an emergency stop. Connected monitoring can feed a PLC or SCADA system and alert staff only when conditions change, freeing personnel from routine checks. True downtime cost should include lost production, labour, wasted material, expedited parts and restart time, not just the replacement part.

The piece has limits. The author works for Watson-Marlow, a pump manufacturer, so the argument favours connected equipment. The figures come from Deloitte, the DOE and industry estimates rather than chemical-plant case studies, and the text ends mid-section on data handling.
