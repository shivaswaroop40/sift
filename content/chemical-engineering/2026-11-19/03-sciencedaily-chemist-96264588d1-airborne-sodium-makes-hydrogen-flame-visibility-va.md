Researchers at Adelaide University found that hydrogen flames can look bright orange one day and nearly invisible the next with identical burner settings. The cause is trace sodium in the air, such as sea salt carried inland. Over two weeks of monitoring, the sodium-related light signal varied by more than 28 times between its lowest and highest values.

Air that had passed over the ocean carried more sodium and gave a stronger orange glow. The team also compared hydrogen with a conventional natural gas cooktop. Hydrogen flames emitted less visible light on average, even in conditions that favoured a brighter flame.

Hydrogen also produces faint blue and red light, but these were easiest to see in darkened surroundings. They are therefore poor indicators under normal lighting. For appliance designers, flame appearance cannot be relied on to show that a burner is lit.

The authors suggest ultraviolet or infrared flame detectors and temperature sensors as alternatives. The work combined laboratory experiments with outdoor tests around Adelaide, and was published in Next Energy. The source gives no detail on detector performance or cost.
