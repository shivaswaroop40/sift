A Nature Chemical Engineering paper examines how inorganic impurities in post-consumer plastic waste affect the catalytic cracking of polyolefins. The authors report that metal contaminants, particularly Na, K, Ca, Mg and Cu, act as zeolite poisons and drive catalyst deactivation during cracking.

Sulfur and chlorine are described as more benign. That runs against the usual concern about halogen and sulfur in plastic waste feeds. The abstract also says the catalyst stays active over extended operation and could give cleaner pyrolysis oils than thermal pyrolysis.

For anyone designing a chemical recycling unit, this points to where feed specification and pretreatment effort should go. Alkali, alkaline earth and copper contamination looks like the main limit on catalyst life, so washing, filtration or guard beds aimed at those metals may matter more than desulfurisation or dechlorination.

The source is only an abstract. It gives no concentrations, deactivation rates, run lengths or oil quality figures, and the cleaner-oil claim is stated as potential rather than demonstrated. Treat the findings as a guide to what to test, not as design numbers.
