Researchers at Nagoya Institute of Technology measured solid-liquid mass transfer in stirred tanks at different mixing powers, using an anion-exchange resin and tracking the liquid's electrical conductivity. Statistical analysis showed the relationship between power and mass transfer was best described by one major turning point. Below it, more power raises transfer. Above it, gains shrink.

Visual observation fixed the turning point to a practical criterion. Effective mixing begins when no particle stays still at the liquid surface for more than 1 to 2 seconds. Particles may still appear briefly at the surface. They need not vanish from it entirely.

For designers, this gives a defined minimum for drawing down light particles, which has been hard to specify. Beyond that point, energy use keeps rising while mass transfer improves less. That supports choosing operating conditions near the transition rather than well above it.

The power needed to reach the transition depended on impeller position and pumping direction. Under the tested conditions, an impeller close to the liquid surface drew particles down more easily and needed less power. The source gives no figures for scale-up, so the practical limits remain untested here.
