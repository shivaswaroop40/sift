A study of Zn/HZSM-5 in LDPE pyrolysis isolates the active Zn species responsible for rapid coke-induced deactivation. Isolated Zn sites drive deep dehydrogenation of hydrocarbons, producing acetylene and diene intermediates that feed polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) formation. Oxygenated additives in the feed decompose on acid sites into intermediates that lower the barrier for PAH condensation, accelerating coking.

The work matters because the same sites that give high selectivity to monocyclic aromatics are the ones that die fastest. Coke forms heterogeneously across the pellet: external surfaces trap long-chain alkenes and oxygenates, while micropores build condensed aromatics through sequential HACA and MAC routes. Progressive coking sinters [ZnOH]+ into inert ZnO, shifting the catalyst from Zn-driven aromatisation towards Brønsted acid cracking and a loss of selectivity.

The authors frame this as an intrinsic trade-off in catalyst design. Any attempt to boost monocyclic aromatic yields through Zn speciation must also manage PAH precursor formation, oxygenate contamination, and micropore accessibility to extend catalyst life in plastic upcycling.
