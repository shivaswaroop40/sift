Chemists at the Leibniz Institute for Catalysis in Rostock have converted polyethylene into dicarboxylic acids, the precursors for nylon. Pieces of a plastic bag dissolve in a test tube of highly dilute nitric acid and hydrogen peroxide, heated to a maximum of 120°C. The reaction needs no catalyst. The target product is a dicarboxylic acid with exactly six carbon atoms.

Polyethylene and polypropylene make up four-fifths of plastic waste, an estimated 400 million tonnes a year. Pyrolysis, the established recycling method, runs at up to 600°C, and its energy demand leaves a CO₂ footprint large enough that US plants are shutting down. Oxidative catalytic alternatives need high pressure, which is explosive and hard to scale, plus precious metal catalysts. Nylon output is about 8 million tonnes a year, with petroleum as the main raw material.

Earlier nitric acid routes used concentrated acid at 180°C in a microwave and produced chain lengths from two to more than 40 carbons, which their authors used as soap surfactants. The Rostock group found that control depends on a specific heat input regime. The conversion runs through several stages, and intermediates left too long yield a complex mixture.

Experiments have narrowed the product chain length to between four and 10 carbons, short of the exact six-carbon target, which places higher demands on selectivity and separation. With Udo Kragl of chemical engineering at the University of Rostock, the group has applied for funding to improve both and to work out the reaction mechanism. A collaborator in Guayaquil is testing how light affects the reactive oxygen species. A paper is being prepared for JACS, with pilot plant scale as the goal.
