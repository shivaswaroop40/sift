Researchers at King's College London and Rutgers chemically modified the surface of a cutinase enzyme and paired it with an ionic liquid. In lab tests on PET powder, the activity rate rose 9.2-fold and the number of reactions rose about 9.5-fold. The modified enzyme kept a steady rate for seven days. The unmodified enzyme in water began losing activity after about 36 hours.

The ionic liquid loosens the packed PET structure and allows operation at high temperature, where PET is easier to attack. The method also worked on flakes from a used beverage bottle without heating or other pretreatment. The team isolated terephthalic acid as a pure powder and recovered 97.1% of the ionic liquid in a lab experiment.

The main caveat is that the enzyme stopped working effectively after the recovery step, so only the liquid could be reused. The study used cutinase, not the more advanced engineered PET enzymes. The estimate that those enzymes could cut near-complete breakdown from about two days to five hours is extrapolated from earlier work, not tested here.

The authors say scale-up needs better enzyme recovery and an assessment of energy demand and overall environmental and economic performance. None of that has been done yet.
