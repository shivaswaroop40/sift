Food processors use chlorine-based disinfectants to reduce pathogens during washing. Research at USC found that chlorine reacts with proteins and fats in food, forming compounds called chlorotyrosines and fatty acid chlorohydrins that remain bound within the food matrix until digestion. Standard contaminant tests may miss these transformation products.

For process engineers, this matters because it reveals a gap between testing protocols and actual chemical exposure. These byproducts form during normal operations, yet their long-term health effects remain unknown. The reactions vary unpredictably between foods: vegetables show high levels after treatment, while meat and fish contain substantial amounts before treatment begins.

The practical trade-off is real. Triple-washing prevents cross-contamination and removes debris effectively, but cannot eliminate pathogens already established in produce. Cutting vegetables ruptures cells, releasing organic matter that consumes disinfectant while generating more byproducts. Simpson notes that using chlorine for appearance rather than safety compounds the risk calculation.
