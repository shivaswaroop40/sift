A study in Chemical Engineering Journal reports that the size of nickel particles and the density of oxygen vacancies at the Ni–Al2O3 interface control which form of carbon deposits on the catalyst during dry reforming of methane. Smaller Ni particles paired with more oxygen vacancies favour filamentous or graphitic coke, while larger particles shift the deposit towards amorphous or encapsulating carbon. This links active site geometry directly to coke speciation rather than just total carbon build-up.

For practising engineers, the result points to a design knob. Tuning metal dispersion and the metal–support interface offers a route to either suppress deactivating encapsulating coke or tolerate filamentous coke that is less harmful to activity. It suggests that dry reforming catalyst formulations can be ranked not only by activity or coking rate, but by the type of carbon they form under reaction conditions.

The evidence comes from controlled Ni–Al2O3 catalysts spanning a range of particle sizes, with oxygen vacancy concentration measured at the interface. The paper claims a direct correlation between these features and the Raman and TEM signatures of the deposited carbon. As the source is an abstract, the quantitative coke rates, time-on-stream data and regeneration behaviour are not given here.
