A team at Institute of Science Tokyo has built an ammonia synthesis catalyst from cobalt on a barium anti-perovskite. The material, Ba₃AlO₄₋ₓNᵧHz, accommodates oxide, nitride, hydride and molecular dinitrogen anions in a flexible crystal lattice. Grown as an ultrathin shell on alumina nanorods, with cobalt clusters self-organising on the outer surface, it reached an ammonia rate of 15.9 mmol per gram of catalyst per hour at 300 °C and 0.9 MPa.

The authors report that this rate surpasses benchmark ruthenium catalysts and industrial iron catalysts. The result matters because ammonia synthesis still needs high temperature and pressure to break the nitrogen-nitrogen bond, and the best mild-condition catalysts depend on scarce noble metals. Barium alternatives such as barium subnitride degrade in hydrogen during operation. A stable, precious-metal-free catalyst working at 300 °C would lower the barrier to ammonia as a carbon-free hydrogen carrier.

Isotope labelling, spectroscopy and simulations point to a mechanism centred on (N₂)⁻ ions at interface sites between cobalt, barium and aluminium. Barium sites weaken the nitrogen-nitrogen bond, so the dinitrogen anion dissociates easily and fresh N₂ quickly replenishes the site. Cobalt and aluminium sites then drive the subsequent steps to ammonia. The authors describe this as a previously unknown ammonia synthesis route mediated by (N₂)⁻ ions.
